# Invariance

**Status:** working research note.

## 1. Working definition
An invariant is a specified property or relation that remains preserved under a specified class of transformations, within declared measurement uncertainty and domain limits.

No claim of invariance is meaningful without naming the transformation.

## 2. Minimum specification
Every invariance claim must state:
1. the system and boundary;
2. the property or relation claimed to be invariant;
3. the transformation applied;
4. the quantities held fixed;
5. the allowed tolerance and measurement uncertainty;
6. the domain and conditions of validity;
7. the observations that would contradict the claim.

## 3. Types to investigate
- State invariance: a state descriptor remains unchanged.
- Relational invariance: a relation among components remains unchanged.
- Temporal invariance: a temporal structure or ordering persists.
- Scale invariance: a relation persists under a defined change of scale.
- Observer invariance: a result remains stable across specified observation procedures.
- Transformational invariance: a rule describing change remains stable across cases.

These are research categories, not claims that such invariants exist in every system.

## 4. Invariance and error
A discrepancy may reveal a genuine breaking of an invariant, an incorrectly specified invariant, an observer or instrument effect, a transition between regimes, or a relation preserved only under a narrower transformation class.

A failed invariance claim should be recorded with its original scope and evidence. Do not silently redefine the invariant after seeing the data.

## 5. Cross-domain comparison
Different physical systems may be compared only after their observables and transformations have been operationally defined. A shared mathematical relation must survive tests against plausible alternatives. Similarity of appearance is not evidence of mathematical equivalence.
